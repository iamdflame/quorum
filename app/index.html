The finding

Privacy is not encryption. It is the number of people you could have been.

A shielded pool encrypts amounts, breaks the link between sender and receiver, and proves it in zero knowledge. None of that helps if you are the only person who did something that looked like what you did.

Cryptography gives you a hiding place. It does not give you a crowd — and the crowd is a separate problem that nobody owns.

Of cells measured on mainnet, contain exactly one participant. The largest crowd anywhere in the entire pool is ten.

Why it fragments

The set splits three ways at once, and none of them are visible to you.

An anonymity set does not fragment once. It fragments along three axes simultaneously, and a user relying on it sees none of them.

Asset. STRK20 groups notes into per-token subchannels, so your crowd is whoever else shielded that token — not whoever else uses the pool.

Denomination. Deposits and withdrawals carry plaintext amounts. Your crowd is whoever else moved roughly that much.

Time. Every edge is stamped by its block. Your crowd is whoever else was moving in that window.

Intersect the three and a pool with hundreds of users offers a crowd of one.

Method

The first measurement was wrong. That is why this one is worth stating.

Raw event data showed 4,550 withdrawals against 945 deposits — an anomaly worth attacking rather than publishing. 68.8% of all withdrawals went to a single address: a deployed contract that never deposits and receives across thirteen assets.

A private swap withdraws to an anonymizer, not to a person. Counting that as a participant invents a crowd out of plumbing.

So infrastructure is now classified from chain state rather than a maintained list. Anonymizers come from the pool's own ExternalContractInvoked events; sinks are deployed contracts that receive across several assets and never deposit. Only an ordinary account or a depositor survives as a participant — erring toward inclusion, so no real person is deleted from the crowd.

The finding held after removing thirty-four such addresses.

Counting people is itself the wrong measure. A hundred operators where one accounts for 99% of the flow is not a crowd of a hundred. These figures are perplexity — 2H over the flow distribution — which equals the headcount when flow is even and collapses toward one as it concentrates.

Not a flaw

The pool is young. Small sets follow from low volume, not bad design.

This is not a break in STRK20. The cryptography does what it claims, and the protocol's own documentation is candid that patterns leak.

Which is precisely the point. Anonymity is the one property you cannot ship on your own — it has to be accumulated, and every new pool, asset and app splits it further.

No amount of cryptographic quality fixes a crowd of one. Somebody has to aggregate the crowd, and nobody is.

Shoal

You don't pick a pool. You pick to be indistinguishable.

Shoal measures the crowd you are actually standing in, then routes value so that crowd is as large as it can be — choosing denominations the pool genuinely uses and windows that are already busy, rather than the fastest path.

Two rules govern it, and both are conjunctive. A plan is only as anonymous as its weakest leg, so its crowd is the minimum across legs and never the average. And every additional leg is another observation to correlate, so splitting is priced rather than assumed good.

That is also the flywheel: routed flow converges on shared denominations and windows, so cells grow, so routing improves, so more flow routes. The product gets better by being used — and it gets better for everyone in the cell, not only the person who routed. It is the one moat in this field that cannot be forked, because forking an anonymity set splits it and makes both halves worse.
